## 1. The Five Psychological Frames

Every tier, every proposal line, every call moment serves one of these. They are universal across clients.

**Frame 1 — The floor must be real, not a strawman.** The $10K tier honestly describes the generic version: *"This is the program as I run it for any team. Same curriculum, same quality, same weekly rhythm. No discovery, no company-specific tailoring."* Savvy buyers smell manufactured contrast — the moment the floor feels fake, the whole structure loses credibility. The buyer reads $10K and silently asks "am I the company this is for?" If they asked for customization, their own brain rules it out. You never argue; they self-select off.

**Frame 2 — The middle tier is what was already scoped.** $12,500 contains exactly what the buyer verbally agreed to during discovery. Nothing new at pricing time. Their brain registers it as confirmation, not a choice: "oh, that's what we talked about" — status-quo bias does the work. The sealing phrase, said aloud AND written in the doc: *"This is the version we scoped together on our [date] call."* The buyer co-authored the price; you're just writing it down. Upstream requirement: verbally scope the middle-tier elements (context files, interviews, guided setup, tailoring) by name on the discovery call.

**Frame 3 — The real risk is under-rolling, not overspending.** The heaviest-lifting frame. The buyer's unconscious fear isn't "I'll pay too much for training" — it's "I'll roll out AI in a way that embarrasses me six months from now": sloppy permissions, data where it shouldn't be, someone hitting something weird with a client file. Surface it without naming it: *"There's a fast way and a right way."* · *"Some people start using the tool in ways you'd rather they didn't."* · *"The structure and safeguards a serious rollout requires."* The buyer feels the fear for three seconds, then sees the answer in the guided-setup line. The sentence in their head (never out loud): *"I'm paying the extra money to say, six months from now, 'we rolled this out the right way, with a professional, and I didn't cowboy it.'"* **Never say "insurance policy" or anything adjacent — the frame works because the buyer completes the thought.**

**Frame 4 — The MIT anchor lifts the room, then you step down.** The script: *"MIT's 2025 study found 95% of enterprise AI efforts stall — not because the tools don't work, but because of the learning gap between having AI and putting it to work. That research is about large enterprises. [Company] isn't that. But the gap is the same one that takes down smaller rollouts."* The most valuable sentence in the entire offer: *"That research is about large enterprises. [Company] isn't that."* It's the moment the buyer decides you're an advisor, not a training vendor. **Warning:** the MIT stat has become a sales-tactic tell. Only use it with the step-down. Otherwise skip it.

**Frame 5 — The top tier is a different buyer, not a premium version.** Middle-tier buyer wants clean execution; doesn't need a dashboard. Top-tier buyer wants accountability built into the structure, a visible before-and-after, and treats it as a capital decision. The mechanic that makes it real: *"It has to be chosen upfront because the baseline is built at kickoff."* It closes when the buyer hears "measurement" and translates it to "I can see who's serious and who isn't" — Joe said it out loud: "we'll know who's putting in the effort, and if they're not, we'll exit them from the program." **Field evidence: this frame is stronger than originally assumed — 2 of the first 3 closes (Joe, Cindy) went top tier.** The top tier is a lightweight performance-management tool wrapped in training. Don't name it; let the buyer arrive at it.

## 2. The Marginal Math

| Step | What the buyer sees | What their brain does |
|------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| 1 | $10K baseline | "That's the floor. But it doesn't include what I asked for. Not mine." |
| 2 | $12,500 | "This is what we scoped. $2,500 more to make it fit my business. Fair." |
| 3 | $15,000 | "Another $2,500 for measurement and the full exec rhythm. Do I want the visible before-and-after?" |

- $10K → $12.5K is **$2,500**, protected by "this is what we scoped." The buyer must argue with themselves to pick $10K.
- $12.5K → $15K is **$2,500** — a symmetric, deliberate step. For accountability-minded operators (the Joes and Cindys), it reads as cheap insurance on a leadership statement, and the field data says they take it.
- **Middle is the default. Top wins when stakes feel bigger than first realized — which is happening more often than not. The floor doesn't win at all; it was never supposed to.**

## 3. The 30-Minute Call Architecture

Pre-call conditions (ALL must be true for a first-call close): middle tier verbally scoped on a prior call · explicit middle-tier asks heard · pricing range already seen/heard · sovereign single decision-maker · action-oriented buyer. Any false → second-call close.

| Minutes | Beat | Notes |
|---------|------|-------|
| 0–2 | Warm open | Personal reconnect; touch any existing coaching work. No pitch. |
| 2–4 | Frame the call | "I put together a proposal based on what we talked about. I'll walk you through it — ~15 minutes — then questions and next steps. Sound good?" ADHD-sensitive: the buyer knows the shape. |
| 4–6 | "What I heard from you" | Read 6–8 bullets of what you heard on discovery. Often closes the deal by itself. |
| 6–7 | The three shifts | 60 seconds max. Anchor in the end state. |
| 7–11 | Walk the program | Timeline first, then each session ~30 seconds. Let the arc carry it. |
| 11–14 | Fast-vs-right + MIT | The loaded section. **Pause five seconds after MIT.** |
| 14–16 | Mechanics | Drumbeat, real work, channel, recordings. Brief, visual. |
| 16–18 | Getting ready / what you provide | Partnership signal. The guided-setup bullet is where Frame 3 lands. |
| 18–22 | Investment | All three tiers honestly. Floor without apology. Middle = "the version we scoped." Top = "for when stakes feel bigger." **Then stop talking.** |
| 22–27 | **The silence** | The most important moment. Buyer speaks first. Count to ten. |
| 27–30 | Close | SOW timing, first payment, kickoff window. Concrete. |

## 4. The Four Paths After the Silence

- **A — picks the middle.** Confirm, send SOW, move on. Do not re-sell.
- **B — "what do you recommend?"** *"$12,500. That's the program we designed together. The top tier is worth the extra $2,500 if you want measurement and the full sponsor rhythm — I'd sleep well either way."* Then wait.
- **C — pushes on price/floor.** Floor: *"Honestly, that version doesn't include the things you asked for. I can run it, and it'll still be a good program, but you'd feel the gap by Session 3. Your call — that's the honest read."* Discount: *"One lever. Full amount upfront via ACH, I can knock $1,000 off. Beyond that I don't discount the program, because the delivery work is the delivery work."*
- **D — goes top tier unprompted.** Don't talk them out of it. *"Good call. The measurement piece is going to make this visible in a way the middle tier wouldn't."* Move on. (This is now the most common path.)

## 5. The Script Library (use verbatim or close to it)

**The open:** "Here's how I want to use our time today. I put together a proposal for the team program based on everything we talked about. I'll share my screen and walk you through it. Should take about 15 minutes. Then we'll leave time for your questions and next steps. Sound good?"

**The $12,500 tier:** "This is the version we scoped together on our [date] call. Two weeks of discovery, the context file, sessions tailored to your actual workflows. The program arrives built for you, not adapted to you."

**The $15,000 tier:** "This is the tier for when you want a visible before-and-after. When you want the program's architecture to do some of the accountability work. It has to be chosen upfront because the baseline is built at kickoff."

**Closing the call:** "I'll send you an SOW today or tomorrow. Once you sign and the first installment clears, we start the two weeks of pre-sprint. I'll reach out to [team lead] to book the setup sessions and to you to schedule discovery calls."

## 7. Buyer Archetypes

- **The Joe** (owner-operator rebuilding deliberately): hot button = leadership self-image, "rolled out AI the right way." Closed at $15K when the accountability frame landed. Trusts his advisor; decides fast.
- **The Dave** (franchisor / multi-location operator): hot button = operational standardization across units without flying people in. Closed at $12K-range with a governance add-on.
- **The Cindy** (second-gen family-business owner / exec right hand): hot button = professionalization — "we do it the right way now." **Closed at $15K** — wanted the paper trail and credibility, exactly as the archetype predicted.
- **The Committee Buyer** (corporate, multi-stakeholder): tell = procurement/legal/MSA/bake-off questions. **Not a fit** — the Sprint assumes sovereign buying. Redirect.

## 8. The Validated Closing Pattern

1. The decision is made before the call — trust built over months; the proposal confirms the buyer was heard, not persuaded.
2. "What I heard from you" → buyer relaxes; you reflected his priorities with more clarity than he had.
3. Program walkthrough → buyer evaluates operational fit ("does this plan make sense"), not value ("should I buy").
4. "No, I think it's pretty clean" before pricing = the tell. No objections = already decided.
5. Pricing walkthrough → buyer discusses payment mechanics ("a third, a third, a third"), not value comparison.
6. Self-selects to top tier — the accountability frame triggers the management instinct.
7. "Let's just start living forward." Timeline confirmed, zero friction.

**Also validated:** the HTML scroll proposal closes before the call — the format is the argument · let buyers pay the card fee for points · PDF + print/scan e-signature fallback · verbal and written price must match exactly.
