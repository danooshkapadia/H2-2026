# HCD Foundations for Learning Design

The methodology underneath every design: IDEO.org's human-centered design (Danoosh's professional lineage) fused with Olivia Vagelos's Design for Feelings school of experience design. Read this when designing research, when a design has drifted content-first, or when you need a method to get unstuck.

## The three phases, mapped to curriculum

HCD: "a process that starts with the people you're designing for and ends with new solutions that are tailor made to suit their needs." Deep empathy → many ideas → many prototypes → sharing with the people you're designing for → launching.

| Phase | In HCD | In curriculum design |
|---|---|---|
| **Inspiration** | Immerse in people's lives, understand needs directly | Treat learners as research subjects before they're students. Interview the cohort and sponsor, mine transcripts and surveys, surface hopes/fears/baseline and the real work the learning must serve, before writing one objective. |
| **Ideation** | Themes → insights → How Might We → brainstorm → prototype | Synthesize research into 3-5 insight statements, reframe the hardest as HMWs, generate many session concepts, prototype activities cheaply before committing. |
| **Implementation** | Live prototyping, pilot, iterate after launch | The first cohort is a live prototype with defined indicators. Session N's feedback formally reshapes session N+1. A curriculum is never done. |

## The seven mindsets (one line each for a curriculum designer)

1. **Empathy** — design from the learner's lived reality, not your subject-matter outline.
2. **Optimism** — treat "our people will never do that" as a solvable constraint.
3. **Iterate, iterate, iterate** — every session run is a draft that feedback improves.
4. **Creative confidence** — the job is building learners' belief they can act, not just teaching content.
5. **Make it** — don't debate a session design in the abstract; build the run of show and put it in front of someone.
6. **Embrace ambiguity** — sit comfortably in "we don't know the right structure yet"; premature certainty kills better answers.
7. **Learn from failure** — a flopped activity is data, not verdict; design cheap-to-fail experiments.

## The twelve methods most useful here

1. **Frame your design challenge** — set the problem at the right altitude before designing ("help the ELT build confident daily AI habits," not "build an AI training").
2. **Interview** — 1:1, in their context, write what they actually say, not what you think they mean. The single highest-leverage input to cohort design.
3. **Extremes and mainstreams** — research the most resistant AND the most advanced learner. A design that works for both works for everyone. Make the extremes feel their voice is critical.
4. **Five whys** — get past "I don't have time" to the real blocker (fear of looking incompetent, no sanctioned practice space).
5. **Find themes → insight statements** — cluster research into 3-5 statements that carry an "aha." Insights become the rationale for every design choice.
6. **How Might We** — reframe each insight as a question that suggests a solution is possible without prescribing one. Broad enough for wild ideas, narrow enough to start.
7. **Brainstorm rules** — defer judgment, wild ideas welcome, build with "and," one conversation, be visual, go for quantity. Use as a design-team method AND a teachable in-session protocol.
8. **Journey map** — plot the learner's arc across the whole program: invitation, arrival, session peaks, between-session valleys, homework, graduation, what they tell colleagues. Design the weak moments deliberately. "The before-feelings matter."
9. **Storyboard / role play** — sketch the session start to finish before building decks; act out the tricky facilitation moment (the resistant exec, the derailing question) before it happens live.
10. **Rapid prototyping** — isolate the single riskiest element (the new exercise, the timing, the framing) and test just that with 2-3 people. "Prototyping isn't about being precious."
11. **Get feedback → integrate and iterate** — the between-sessions loop. "A method for refining your idea, not getting to the ultimate solution the first time."
12. **Co-creation** — bring learners into the design itself: co-design later modules, let participants facilitate. Contribution converts attendees into a community.

## Experience design: the four principles (IDEO U, Gottstein & Vagelos)

"Experience design is the intentional shaping of how people feel, think, and act during an interaction."

1. **Include your audience: design for feelings, not logistics.** "It is exceptionally hard to inspire change in someone when you don't know anything about them." The key inversion: **start with the feelings you want to inspire, and figure out the activities later.** Most designers start with activities.
2. **Design a powerful opening moment.** The beginning sets the tone and the rules of engagement, and helps people transition in from wherever they came from, physically and emotionally. Game-design language: **entering the magic circle.** Engineer a low-risk first participation within minutes; "clicking unmute is intimidating, and the longer you go without doing it, the harder it gets."
3. **Design a memorable closing moment.** You can't design every second, and you don't need to: people remember peaks and endings (**peak-end rule**). Close with a callback to the opening, reflection, and gratitude. "Closing moments stick."
4. **Prototype your experience.** Focus rehearsal on the moments that matter (openings, closings, the riskiest new element). Run **Very Bad Run-Thrus**: practicing badly a few times cuts the terrible ideas before they reach the real room.

## Design for Feelings (Olivia Vagelos)

Her organizing question: **"How do we want people to feel?"** When you design for the feelings, you don't just plan events, you create the conditions for transformation.

- **Emotional objectives alongside learning objectives.** Anchor every design in a desired feeling. Map the emotional and energetic journey of participants across time as rigorously as the content arc. Clarify **the core shift** at the heart of the experience; it guides every decision.
- **Experience as architecture; thresholds.** "Experience design is an architectural craft: building containers of different shapes and textures, joined by thresholds and portals, with time layered on." The facilitator escorts people between containers: building the path, leaving the signs, holding hands at thresholds. Choose a **structural metaphor** (an expedition, a kitchen, a climb) and let it drive naming, artifacts, environment, and detail end to end.
- **Loops, not lines.** "Experiences that are straight lines are rarely interesting nor particularly powerful. We are built to return."
  - **Repetition** repeats an *element*: mantras, frameworks, repeat-after-me. Builds muscle memory and acts as experiential underlining ("this is important").
  - **Routine** repeats an *architecture*: the identical session skeleton tunes people OUT of what's the same so they tune IN to what's different, freeing brain-RAM for the new. Caveat: pure routine kills memory; attach one novel element to each repeat.
  - **Ritual** is repetition made sacred: the *always* opening warm-up, the *every-time* closing circle. Signal words: every, always, each. "Repetition is practicing. Routine is practical. Ritual is a practice."
- **Structure enables play.** "Wild imagination requires intentional architecture." Rules, roles, timeboxes, and formats are what make risk-taking feel safe. Design explicitly for the trust and safety needed for true play.
- **Door handles: little frictions read as lack of care.** Unclear logistics, missing links, "where do I go?" moments cluster at transitions and add up to an experience that lacks a feeling of care. The inverse is the point: fixing small frictions is felt as caring, and felt care changes how everything else lands.
- **Sync time as multiplier.** Move content consumption to async; "resist the urge to be at the front of your classroom presenting." Live time is only for what can only happen together: working the sticky parts, collaboration, collective creation. "Lead with relationships, not content."
- **Learn-by-living.** Anchor exercises in learners' real work, tools, and environments. Practice in situ makes learning personal and leaves ever-present reminders.
- **Engage the senses.** Music, physical artifacts, movement, embodied metaphors, especially online. "Lead with the beauty, follow with the math." (Paul Bennett, IDEO)
- **Community as co-creators.** Invite learners in as co-collaborators; share power visibly; one person's win becomes the group's knowledge.
